## Review of the WIP commit (step A)

What it did right:

- The mathematics of an exactness test: x^y is a double for x = 2^p and an
  integer p·y in [−1074, 1023]. For x = c^(2^k)·2^(f·2^k) (c odd) and
  y = a/2^k it is a double where c^a < 2^53. A bounded search follows from
  that: y is a multiple of 1/32 in (0, 33] for m ≥ 3, and 1024·y is an
  integer for a power of two. The test used integers and exact square roots,
  and it noticed that the product p*y of doubles cannot decide (8 to the
  double nearest 1/3).
- Tests in `tests/core_math.cpp` against CORE-MATH called in both directions,
  and updates to the tests and documents that showed the old width.

What was wrong or weak:

1. For powers of two it returned the lower bound `w`, CORE-MATH's value, and
   never computed or checked the power it had proved. For m ≥ 3 it compared
   `d == w`. The lower bound therefore depended on CORE-MATH being exact at
   exact cases, when an exactly computed value was already there.
2. The overflow check relied on `std::ldexp()` overflowing to +∞. That
   happens in the upward direction; elsewhere the C library may return the
   largest double. Only the `d == w` comparison caught it.
3. The random pairs of its test were drawn inside the arguments of one call:
   `add(f(gen()), g(gen()))`. The evaluation order of arguments is
   unspecified, so the pairs, and the counts quoted in `doc/tests.md` and
   `doc/accuracy.md` (31 313 and 1 961), depended on the compiler.
4. No test at the edges: no subnormal base or power with m ≥ 3, no power
   `c^a·2^(f·a)` beyond the largest double with f·a ≤ 1023, no exponent f·a
   just below −1074, and no guard that the pairs contain exact powers at all.
5. The manual's example output conflicted with point 13/16 (`<2, 2>` against
   the new `[2]`). The merge resolved it.

## What was wrong and what the change does

`pow(x, y)` on a box with finite bounds takes CORE-MATH's pow at two corners,
correctly rounded upward (point 1, `pow_standard()`). The upper bound is that
value. The lower bound was always the double below it, even where x^y is a
double: `pow([4], 0.5)` was `[2 − 2^-52, 2]`, and special cases 149 to 152 and
163 were wider than IEEE 1788's result.

`gaol/gaol_interval.cpp`:

- `pow_is_double(x, y, d)` decides whether x^y is a double and returns it in
  `d`. It uses integers and floating-point operations that are exact in every
  rounding direction (scaling by 1024, int conversions, bit reads, `sqrt` of a
  perfect square below 2^53, products of integers compared with 2^53, `ldexp`
  of a representable value). The decision is therefore independent of the
  rounding direction, of `FLT_EVAL_METHOD` and of the platform:
  - y = a/2^k (a odd, or k = 0) and x = m·2^e (m odd).
  - m = 1: x^y = 2^(e·y). This is a double iff 1024·y is an integer j,
    e·j is a multiple of 1024, and e·j/1024 is in [−1074, 1023]
    (|e| ≤ 1074 < 2^11, so k ≤ 10).
  - m ≥ 3: x^y is rational only where x = z^(2^k), with z = c·2^f and c odd.
    It is a double iff c^a < 2^53, f·a ≥ −1074 and c^a·2^(f·a) < 2^1024.
    Then 3 ≤ c gives a ≤ 33 and 2^k ≤ 32. The test checks that 2^k divides e,
    that m is a 2^k-th power (k exact square roots, `r*r == m`), that c^a is
    an exact product below 2^53, and the range of the exponent. The largest
    exponent is read from the bits of c^a, not from `ldexp`'s overflow.
  - In the exact case, `d` is computed exactly (`ldexp` of a representable
    value) and does not depend on CORE-MATH.
- `pow_lo()` returns `d` where `pow_is_double()` holds. Elsewhere it returns
  the double below CORE-MATH's value, as before. In the exact case this saves
  a CORE-MATH call.
- `odd_significand()` now reads x from its bits (as the WIP did) and has moved
  before `pow_is_double()`; `hypot()` still uses it. The old version called
  `std::frexp()` and `std::ldexp()` and looped over the trailing zeros, which
  made `pow_is_double()` cost 27 ns for an integer exponent, as much as
  CORE-MATH's pow.
- Cost measured with clang-18 on this Xeon, against about 30 ns for
  CORE-MATH's pow:
  - `pow_is_double()` costs about 2 ns for a non-integer exponent. It leaves at
    the first tests: the range, then 1024·y an integer.
  - For an integer exponent it costs about 4 to 8 ns, with GCC and with
    clang. A cheap rejection helps here: m ≥ 2^27 with y ≥ 2 gives
    c^a ≥ 2^54.
- Comments of `pow_standard()`: why the degenerate integer exponents beyond
  the ints can never be exact (except at 0 and 1, handled apart), and what
  the lower bound at the corners now is.

## Behaviour change

- The lower bound of `pow(x, y)`, `pow(x, d)` (non-integer d) and
  `gaol_ieee1788::pow` changes on boxes with finite bounds. At the corner
  that gives the lower bound, when x^y is a double, that bound is now x^y
  instead of the double below: `pow([4], 0.5)` is `[2]`,
  `pow([3, 4], [2, 3])` is `[9, 64]`, and `pow([4], -0.5)` is `[0.5]`.
- Every other result is bit for bit the same. The upper bound, the integer
  powers, and the exp(y·log x) path for infinite bounds are unchanged.
- The output of such a result changes: `cout << pow(interval(4), 0.5)` prints
  `[2]`, where it printed `[1.999999999999999, 2]`.

  - The repeated checks of point 2 are in the fallback `exp(y*log(base))`
    (an infinite bound, or a base from 0 with y ≤ 0). There are two ways:
    - (a) Write the bodies of `log()`, `exp()` and `*` without the check, as
      TODO.md says.
    - (b) Extend the corner analysis to infinite bounds and to a base from 0
      with the limits of x^y, which removes the fallback. The finite corners
      of those boxes then go through `pow_lo()` and `pow_is_double()`:
      `pow([4, +oo], 0.5)`, now `[2 − 2^-52, +oo]`, would become `[2, +oo]`.
      This needs the expected values of `tests/ieee1788.cpp` (boxes
      `I(0.25, 0.5) × I(-oo, -1)` and `I(2, 4) × I(1, oo)`, which expect
      `0x1.fffffffffffffp+0`) to be updated, and limit tests.
  - Either way, `pow_is_double()` must stay before the CORE-MATH call in
    `pow_lo()`. `pow_exact_at_corners()` (core_math) and `pow_on_boxes()`
    (ieee1788, bit for bit) are the regression net.

## Risks

- Soundness: a wrong `true` from `pow_is_double()` would give a lower bound
  above the power. The test is a proof with exact operations, and it does not
  depend on the rounding direction or on excess precision. It is checked
  against CORE-MATH in both directions on 81 422 pairs in ctest and 62
  million in the stress run. The six broken variants A to F each make the
  test fail.
- 32-bit x86 (Debian i386, Windows x86) was not run here; the CI covers it.
  The code has no `long`, no 64-bit division, and every double conversion is
  of a value below 2^53.
- Cost: about 2 ns per lower corner, and 4 to 8 ns where the exponent bound
  is an integer (against about 30 ns for CORE-MATH's pow). An exact corner
  saves the CORE-MATH call.
